Egyptian hibiscus drink

Start here: karkadeh, a tart ruby-red drink you can serve hot or pour over ice. No extras — just the flowers, water, and something to sweeten it.

Egyptian hibiscus drink (karkadeh)

Karkadeh is a classic Egyptian drink made from dried hibiscus flowers. It is sharp and fruity, a deep ruby red, and often sweetened to balance the tartness. Serve it hot in cooler weather, or chill it and pour it over ice. A little lemon, mint, or a cinnamon stick is enough if you want to dress it up.

  • Serves 4–6
  • 20 minutes, plus chilling if you want it cold

Ingredients

  • 40g dried hibiscus flowers (karkadeh) — about 2 generous handfuls
  • 1.5 litres water
  • 4–8 tablespoons sugar, to taste (or honey)
  • Optional: 1 cinnamon stick, 2 cloves, the juice of half a lemon, and a few fresh mint leaves to serve

Method

  1. Rinse the dried hibiscus flowers in a sieve under cold water.
  2. Bring the water to a boil in a saucepan. Add the flowers, and the cinnamon stick or cloves if you are using them.
  3. Turn the heat down and simmer for 10–15 minutes, until the liquid is a deep ruby red.
  4. Take the pan off the heat. Stir in the sugar while the drink is still hot, so it dissolves. Start with 4 tablespoons, taste, and add more if you like it sweeter — karkadeh is often on the sweet side, to balance the tart flowers.
  5. Strain into a jug, pressing the flowers gently. Stir in the lemon juice if you are using it.
  6. Serve hot in cups, or leave it to cool, then chill and pour over ice. Add a mint sprig if you like.

Ginger, lemon and mint tea

A bright pot of tea: hot ginger, plenty of lemon, and mint leaves crushed in your hands so the oils come through.

  • Serves 2
  • 10 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • A 4cm piece of fresh ginger, sliced
  • 1 lemon — 3 or 4 slices, plus a squeeze at the end
  • A small handful of fresh mint leaves
  • 500ml freshly boiled water
  • Honey, to taste (optional)

Method

  1. Put the ginger and lemon slices in a teapot or heatproof jug.
  2. Pour over the boiled water. Lightly crush the mint in your hands and add it.
  3. Leave to steep for 5–8 minutes. The longer it sits, the stronger the ginger.
  4. Strain into cups. Add a squeeze of lemon, and honey if you want it a little sweeter.

Chamomile and honey tea

A simple cup of chamomile, gently floral, with a little honey stirred through while it is still warm.

  • Serves 2
  • 8 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons dried chamomile flowers, or 2 chamomile tea bags
  • 500ml freshly boiled water
  • 1–2 teaspoons honey
  • Optional: a strip of lemon peel, or a thin slice of orange

Method

  1. Put the chamomile and the lemon peel or orange, if using, in a teapot or jug.
  2. Pour over the boiled water and steep for 5 minutes.
  3. Strain into cups and stir in the honey while the tea is hot, so it dissolves.

Rosemary and thyme roast potatoes

Golden roast potatoes tossed with olive oil, garlic, rosemary and thyme, so the herbs cling to the rough edges and toast in the oven.

  • Serves 4
  • 50 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 1kg floury potatoes (Maris Piper or similar), peeled and cut into chunks
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 sprigs fresh rosemary, leaves chopped
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme, leaves stripped
  • 3 garlic cloves, lightly crushed
  • Sea salt and black pepper

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 200°C (180°C fan).
  2. Parboil the potatoes in salted water for 8–10 minutes, until the edges are just starting to soften. Drain well, then shake the pan to rough up the edges.
  3. Tip into a roasting tin with the oil, rosemary, thyme, garlic, salt and pepper. Toss so every piece is coated.
  4. Roast for 35–40 minutes, turning once, until the outsides are crisp and golden and the herbs smell toasted and fragrant.
  5. Serve hot, with a little extra salt if they need it.

Tomato and basil pasta

A quick pan sauce of ripe tomatoes and garlic, finished with a generous handful of basil so the plate smells green and peppery.

  • Serves 2
  • 20 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 200g spaghetti or linguine
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 2 garlic cloves, thinly sliced
  • 400g ripe tomatoes, chopped — or one 400g tin of good chopped tomatoes
  • A large handful of fresh basil leaves
  • Sea salt and black pepper
  • Optional: a pinch of chilli flakes, and grated hard cheese to serve

Method

  1. Cook the pasta in plenty of salted boiling water until just tender. Keep a mug of the cooking water.
  2. Meanwhile, warm the oil in a wide pan over a medium heat. Add the garlic, and the chilli if you are using it, and cook for about a minute, until it smells sweet but has not browned.
  3. Add the tomatoes and a pinch of salt. Cook for 8–10 minutes, until they break down into a loose sauce. If you are using tinned tomatoes, let them thicken slightly.
  4. Tear in most of the basil. Tip in the drained pasta and a splash of cooking water. Toss until the sauce clings to the strands.
  5. Serve with the rest of the basil, black pepper, and cheese if you like.

Garlic, rosemary and thyme mushrooms on toast

Mushrooms fried until they take on colour, then finished with garlic, rosemary, thyme and a squeeze of lemon.

  • Serves 2
  • 15 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 300g mixed or chestnut mushrooms, sliced
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil, or a knob of butter
  • 2 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 1 sprig rosemary, leaves chopped
  • 3 sprigs thyme, leaves stripped
  • A squeeze of lemon
  • 2 thick slices of bread, toasted
  • Sea salt and black pepper
  • Optional: a little chopped parsley to finish

Method

  1. Heat the oil or butter in a frying pan over a medium-high heat. Add the mushrooms and a pinch of salt. Leave them to brown before stirring, so they get some colour.
  2. When they have given off their liquid and started to turn gold, add the garlic, rosemary and thyme. Cook for another 2 minutes.
  3. Add a squeeze of lemon, black pepper, and a little more salt if it needs it.
  4. Pile onto the toast. Finish with parsley if you have it.

Blends

Smoothies and blended drinks that pair fruit with herbs and spice — mango with mint, strawberry with basil, pineapple with ginger, berries with hibiscus, banana with cinnamon.

Mango and mint

A thick, sunny blend of ripe mango and fresh mint, sharpened with lime. It should taste sweet first, then minty.

  • Serves 1–2
  • 5 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 1 large ripe mango, peeled and chopped (about 250g)
  • A small handful of fresh mint leaves
  • Juice of 1 lime
  • 150ml cold water or coconut water
  • A handful of ice
  • Optional: 2 tablespoons plain yogurt

Method

  1. Put everything in a blender.
  2. Blend until completely smooth.
  3. Taste. If it is too sweet, add a little more lime. If it is too thick, loosen with a splash of cold water.
  4. Pour into a cold glass and drink straight away.

Strawberry and basil

Ripe strawberries, blended with just enough basil that you notice it — a sweet, peppery note, not a pesto.

  • Serves 1–2
  • 5 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 250g ripe strawberries, hulled
  • 6–8 fresh basil leaves
  • Juice of half a lemon
  • 100ml cold water
  • A handful of ice
  • 1 teaspoon honey or sugar, only if the berries are sharp

Method

  1. Put the strawberries, basil, lemon juice, water and ice in a blender. Add the honey or sugar only if the fruit tastes sharp.
  2. Blend until pink and smooth.
  3. Taste, and add a basil leaf or a little more lemon if you want the flavour louder.
  4. Pour and serve at once.

Pineapple and ginger

Juicy pineapple and fresh ginger, with lime to keep it sharp. The ginger should come through at the end, not take over.

  • Serves 1–2
  • 5 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 300g ripe pineapple, chopped (fresh or frozen)
  • A 2cm piece of fresh ginger, peeled
  • Juice of 1 lime
  • 100ml cold water
  • A handful of ice

Method

  1. Put everything in a blender and blend until smooth.
  2. Taste. If the ginger is too strong, add more pineapple. If it needs a lift, add a little more lime.
  3. Pour into a glass and serve straight away.

Berry and hibiscus

Chilled karkadeh blended with mixed berries — deep red, tart from the hibiscus, with the sweetness of the fruit coming through.

  • Serves 1–2
  • 5 minutes, plus cooling the drink
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 200g mixed berries (strawberries, raspberries, blackberries — fresh or frozen)
  • 200ml chilled karkadeh, unsweetened or only lightly sweetened
  • A handful of ice
  • 1 teaspoon honey, only if you want it sweeter

Method

  1. If you do not already have karkadeh made, steep 1 tablespoon of dried hibiscus flowers in 200ml of hot water for 10 minutes, then strain and leave to cool completely.
  2. Put the berries, chilled karkadeh, ice and honey, if using, in a blender.
  3. Blend until deep red and smooth.
  4. Taste, and add a little more honey only if the berries and hibiscus are too sharp for you. Serve at once.

Banana and cinnamon

Ripe bananas blended with milk and cinnamon until it tastes like a cold banana milkshake, with the spice sitting on the finish. Use very ripe bananas — that is where the sweetness comes from.

  • Serves 1–2
  • 5 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 2 ripe bananas
  • ½ teaspoon ground cinnamon, or a little more to taste
  • 200ml cold milk, oat milk or almond milk
  • A handful of ice
  • Optional: 1 teaspoon honey or 1 soft date, and a pinch of vanilla

Method

  1. Put the bananas, cinnamon, milk, ice and any honey, date or vanilla in a blender.
  2. Blend until thick and creamy.
  3. Taste, and add a pinch more cinnamon if you want it warmer.
  4. Pour and serve at once.

Folk tradition

Traditional herbs

Two teas from older folk traditions, made to taste good — with honey, citrus and cinnamon. We only describe how they have traditionally been used. Folk tradition says many things about them; we do not claim that those uses work.

These are recipes, not medical advice. If you take medication, check with a doctor or pharmacist before trying herbal teas.

Damiana tea

A warm, slightly herbal cup rounded with honey, orange and a little cinnamon. Damiana can taste a bit bitter on its own, so the citrus and honey are there to make it pleasant.

Damiana has traditionally been used in Mexico and Central America, including by the Maya, as a romance or love tea. That is a folk tradition, not a claim that the drink does anything.

  • Serves 1–2
  • 10 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 1–2 teaspoons dried damiana leaves
  • 250–300ml freshly boiled water
  • 1–2 teaspoons honey
  • A strip of orange peel, or a thin slice of orange
  • Optional: a small cinnamon stick, a squeeze of lime, or a thin slice of fresh ginger

Method

  1. Put the damiana, orange peel and the cinnamon or ginger, if you are using them, in a teapot or mug.
  2. Pour over the boiled water. Steep for 5–8 minutes. Taste after 5 minutes — damiana can turn bitter if it sits too long.
  3. Strain. Stir in the honey while the tea is hot, and add a squeeze of lime or orange if you want it brighter.
  4. Serve warm.

Mugwort tea

An aromatic, slightly bitter herbal tea, softened with honey, lemon and cinnamon so it drinks smoothly rather than harshly.

Mugwort tea has a long history of use in European and Asian folk tradition. Folk tradition says many things about it. This is a recipe, not a claim that those uses work.

Warning: Do not drink mugwort tea if you are pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding. Avoid it if you are allergic to plants in the daisy or ragweed family (including chamomile). If you take medication, check with a doctor or pharmacist before trying herbal teas.

  • Serves 1
  • 8 minutes
Ingredients and method

Ingredients

  • 1 teaspoon dried mugwort leaves — do not use more, as the flavour turns bitter quickly
  • 250ml freshly boiled water
  • 1–2 teaspoons honey
  • A slice of lemon, or a strip of lemon peel
  • Optional: a small cinnamon stick, or a thin slice of orange

Method

  1. Put the mugwort, lemon and the cinnamon or orange, if you are using them, in a mug or teapot.
  2. Pour over the boiled water. Steep for 4–6 minutes and taste early — mugwort gets bitter if it steeps too long.
  3. Strain well. Stir in the honey while the tea is hot, and add a little more lemon if you want it brighter.
  4. Serve warm.

About

Recipes for Health is a small collection of herb-led recipes from Hoodlum Asylum. Everything here starts with flavour: hibiscus, mint, ginger, rosemary, basil, thyme, chamomile, the fruit you blend with them, and a couple of teas from folk tradition.

These are recipes, not medical advice. We share how to make drinks and dishes that taste good. We do not claim they treat, cure, prevent or heal anything. Where we mention folk tradition, we are describing a history of use, not saying those uses work.

If you take medication, check with a doctor or pharmacist before trying herbal teas. Mugwort tea has its own warning on the recipe: do not drink it if you are pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding, and avoid it if you are allergic to the daisy or ragweed family.

If a recipe uses honey, do not give it to children under 12 months.
